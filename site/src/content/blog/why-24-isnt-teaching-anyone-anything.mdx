Ask a plain answer engine for the area of the smaller triangle formed by connecting a triangle's centroid to two of its vertices, and you'll usually get a number back fast. Correct, even. And almost useless for actually learning anything.

Here's the same question, worked the way a real explanation should:

1. The centroid divides each of the triangle's three medians in a 2:1 ratio.
2. That ratio means the centroid splits the original triangle into six smaller triangles of **equal area**.
3. The triangle in question is made of two of those six equal pieces.
4. So its area is $\frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$ of the original triangle's area.
5. If the original area is 72, the answer is $72 \times \frac{1}{3} = 24$.

Both paths land on 24. Only one of them teaches you why 24 is the answer — and more importantly, why the *next* centroid problem you see, with different numbers, still reduces to "how many of the six equal pieces am I looking at."

## The number was never the hard part

Most math questions aren't hard because arithmetic is hard. They're hard because it isn't obvious which property of the shape actually matters. A calculator can't tell you that; it can only tell you the answer once you've already figured that part out yourself. That's the actual gap between "get an answer" and "understand a problem" — and it's the gap this product exists to close.

## Why the diagram matters as much as the steps

Step 2 above — "the centroid splits the triangle into six equal-area pieces" — is a genuinely non-obvious geometric fact. Reading it as a sentence is one thing. *Watching* the six pieces get marked out on the actual triangle, in real time, while the explanation is being narrated, is a different kind of understanding entirely. That's the whole thesis behind drawing the diagram live instead of pasting in a static image: the diagram isn't decoration on top of the explanation, it's half of the explanation.
