Every right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$ satisfies:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

That's the version everyone memorizes. It's also, on its own, not very illuminating — it tells you *what's* true without telling you *why*.

## One way to see it

Take a right triangle and build a square directly on each of its three sides. The Pythagorean theorem is really a statement about **area**: the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the combined area of the squares on the two legs.

For a 6-8-10 triangle, that means:

$$
6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2
$$

The squares aren't just an illustration of the formula — they're the reason the formula is true in the first place. There are over 350 known proofs of this theorem, and a good number of them are literally about cutting up and rearranging exactly these squares to show the areas match.

## Why this matters beyond the formula itself

Once you've actually seen why the relationship holds — not just memorized the equation — a whole class of problems stops looking unfamiliar. A ladder leaning against a wall, the diagonal of a rectangular screen, the straight-line distance between two points on a coordinate grid — every one of these is the same right-triangle relationship wearing a different costume. Recognizing the shape underneath a word problem is the actual skill; the formula is just the last step, once you've spotted it.

That recognition is exactly what a diagram-first explanation is built to develop — not by handing you the formula faster, but by making the shape of the problem visible before the arithmetic even starts.
